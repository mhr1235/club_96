## Chunk: sweet_tea_south_as_contradiction

Source: E. Patrick Johnson, Sweet Tea: Black Gay Men of the South, introduction
URL: local:/Users/markramos/Downloads/training corpora 2/bob/oral history book queer black men in the south/9780807882733_johnson.4.pdf
Tags: Bob, Sweet Tea, Black gay South, oral history, southernness, community, contradiction

Johnson frames the South as a contradictory place: shaped by racial violence, religious conservatism, gentility, family obligation, and everyday neighborliness. Black gay Southern life emerges within those contradictions, not outside them. Bob can use this to avoid flat assumptions that Southern queer life is only repression or only resilience.

## Chunk: sweet_tea_hidden_in_plain_sight

Source: E. Patrick Johnson, Sweet Tea: Black Gay Men of the South, introduction
URL: local:/Users/markramos/Downloads/training corpora 2/bob/oral history book queer black men in the south/9780807882733_johnson.4.pdf
Tags: Bob, Sweet Tea, hidden in plain sight, coded speech, privacy, respectability, Southern queer history

Johnson describes Black gay Southern stories as often "hidden in plain sight" because sexuality, like other private or taboo matters, may be managed through tacit knowledge, silence, humor, gossip, and codes of respectability. Bob can use this when building Club 96 norms: not every guest will want visibility, naming, or announcement, but that does not mean they are absent.

## Chunk: sweet_tea_oral_history_as_archive

Source: E. Patrick Johnson, Sweet Tea: Black Gay Men of the South, introduction
URL: local:/Users/markramos/Downloads/training corpora 2/bob/oral history book queer black men in the south/9780807882733_johnson.4.pdf
Tags: Bob, Sweet Tea, oral history, archives, Black queer memory, documentation, living archive

Johnson treats oral history as vital because formal archives and historians have often neglected Black queer Southern lives. Bob can use this as a reminder that Club 96 documentation should include interviews, everyday stories, and trusted local narrators.

## Chunk: sweet_tea_get_down_code

Source: E. Patrick Johnson, Sweet Tea: Black Gay Men of the South, "Do You Get Down?: Homosex in the South"
URL: local:/Users/markramos/Downloads/training corpora 2/bob/oral history book queer black men in the south/9780807882733_johnson.8.pdf
Tags: Bob, Sweet Tea, get down, coded language, Black vernacular, desire, sexual identity

Johnson explains "Do you get down?" as Black vernacular code that could signal same-sex desire without requiring a public gay identity. The phrase shows how language can protect privacy, create possibility, and distinguish behavior from labels. Bob can apply this by listening for local language rather than forcing Club 96 guests into imported identity scripts.

## Chunk: sweet_tea_desire_beyond_metropolis

Source: E. Patrick Johnson, Sweet Tea: Black Gay Men of the South, introduction
URL: local:/Users/markramos/Downloads/training corpora 2/bob/oral history book queer black men in the south/9780807882733_johnson.4.pdf
Tags: Bob, Sweet Tea, rural queer life, Southern queer history, Black gay community, place

Sweet Tea challenges the idea that vital queer subcultures only flourish in northern, secular, urban places. Johnson's narrators show Black gay community-building and desire emerging within and against Southern culture, including rural towns, churches, homes, and local networks. Bob can use this to value small, ordinary social spaces as much as big-city nightlife.
